# Usage, form, and commonly misused words

The proofreading step of both passes in [SKILL.md](SKILL.md): the guidance in Chapters I, III, and IV of *The Elements of Style*, restated in our own words and examples. Advice that no longer fits modern or technical usage is adapted; the changes are listed at the end.

## Punctuation and grammar (Chapter I)

1. **Singular possessives** take *'s* whatever the final letter: *James's turret*, *the boss's call*. Exceptions: *Jesus'*, ancient names ending in *-es* or *-is*, and fixed phrases like *for goodness' sake*. Possessive pronouns (*its, hers, theirs, yours, ours*) take no apostrophe; *it's* means "it is". Indefinite pronouns do take one: *someone's grid*.
2. **Serial comma.** In a list of three or more joined by one *and* or *or*, put a comma after every item but the last: *iron, nickel, and silicon*. Use it even where the surrounding document leaves it out. Company names follow the company's own style.
3. **Interrupting phrases get a comma on both sides**, never just one: *The convoy, if left alone, stays on the road.* If the interruption is slight (*however*, one short phrase), both commas may go.
   - Commas also set off names in direct address, *etc., i.e., e.g.*, degrees and titles after a name (*Ana Ruiz, PhD,*), and the year in *May 4, 2026,* mid-sentence. *4 May 2026* takes no comma.
   - No comma before a defining label: *the modder Ana*, *Jack the Ripper*. *Jr.* takes no comma.
   - **Restrictive** clauses identify which one and take no commas: *Grids that lose power despawn.* **Nonrestrictive** clauses add a fact and take commas: *The Bomber, which flies at 400 m, ignores ground targets.* The same applies to participle phrases and appositives.
   - Put a comma after an introductory phrase or clause before the main clause.
4. **Comma before a conjunction** (*and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet*, and *while* meaning "at the same time") that joins two complete clauses. If a dependent clause sits right after the conjunction, the comma goes before the conjunction, not after it. With one shared subject: keep the comma before *but*, drop it before *and* when the two actions are closely tied (*He read the log and found the error*).
5. **No comma splices.** Two complete sentences join with a semicolon, a period, or a comma plus conjunction. The semicolon is often best: it shows the two statements are linked, usually as cause and effect. *However, therefore, then, besides, thus* between clauses still need a semicolon. Very short, parallel clauses may take a comma: *We came, we built, we left.*
6. **No sentence fragments** punctuated as sentences, unless the emphasis is clearly intended (mainly in dialogue).
7. **Colon** after a complete clause, to introduce a list, an appositive, an explanation, or a quotation. Never between a verb and its object, or a preposition and its object: *needs three parts: a rotor, a stator, and a cap*, not *needs: a rotor…*. A colon can join two clauses when the second explains the first. It also follows a formal salutation, and separates hour from minute, title from subtitle, and chapter from verse.
8. **Dash** for an abrupt break or to introduce a long appositive or summary, and only when a comma, colon, or parentheses won't do.
9. **Subject-verb agreement.** The subject decides the verb; words in between don't change it.
   - *One of the encounters that **spawn** at night*: the verb agrees with *encounters*.
   - *Each, either, everyone, everybody, neither, nobody, someone* take a singular verb. *None* is singular when it means "not one", plural when it means "not any of them".
   - Subjects joined by *and* take a plural verb, except set phrases treated as one thing (*rock and roll is*) and subjects led by *each* or *every*.
   - *With, as well as, together with, in addition to, except* do not make a singular subject plural.
   - A linking verb agrees with its subject: *The problem is the drills.* Some plural-looking nouns are singular (*physics is*).
10. **Pronoun case.**
    - Subject forms for subjects: *Mia and I stayed*, never *myself* or *yourself* as a subject. *Who* or *whom* depends on its role inside its own clause: *the player who we think will win* (he will win), *the player whom we hope to recruit* (recruit him).
    - Object forms after verbs and prepositions: *between you and me*.
    - In comparisons, add the understood verb: *more than I do*, *more than she loves me*.
    - A gerund usually takes the possessive: *They objected to **our** flying low.*
11. **Dangling modifiers.** An opening participle or descriptive phrase must describe the subject that follows. *Flying low, the radar missed the drone* says the radar was flying. Recast: *Flying low, the drone slipped under the radar.*

## Form (Chapter III)

- **Slang**: if you use it, use it plainly, without quotation marks around it.
- **Exclamation marks** only for real exclamations or commands, never to pump up a plain statement.
- **Headings and titles** take no final period; a question mark is fine if the heading asks a question.
- **Hyphens** join a compound adjective before a noun: *a long-range turret*, but *the turret is long range*. Don't hyphenate words better written as one; a dictionary decides. A hyphen joins only the words it touches.
- **Numbers**: dates, rule numbers, versions, and serial numbers go in figures. In dialogue, spell most numbers out.
- **Parentheses**: punctuate the sentence as though the parenthetical weren't there. Inside, punctuate the parenthetical as its own text but drop its final period (keep a question or exclamation mark). A whole sentence in parentheses takes its period inside.
- **Quotations**:
  - A formal quotation used as evidence follows a colon. A quotation that is the object of a verb follows a comma.
  - In prose, commas and periods go inside the closing quotation mark.
  - A long quotation goes on its own indented lines, without quotation marks.
  - *that* plus paraphrase (indirect speech) takes no quotation marks; proverbs and well-known phrases need none either.
  - In technical text, keep literal strings, commands, and IDs exactly as typed, in code formatting, so outside punctuation can't be mistaken for part of them.
- **References**: put citations in parentheses or footnotes rather than in the sentence itself. Abbreviate frequently cited titles and list the full forms at the end. Follow the required style guide (APA, MLA, and so on) when there is one.
- **Titles of works** go in italics. Drop a leading *A* or *The* after a possessive: *Tolkien's Hobbit*.
- **Word breaks** at line ends follow the dictionary's syllables.

## Commonly misused words (Chapter IV)

Often the fix is to replace a vague phrase with a specific statement, not to swap one word for another.

| Word or phrase | Use |
| --- | --- |
| affect / effect | *Affect*: to influence. *Effect*: a result (noun) or to bring about (verb). Vague arty *effects* hide the real description. |
| aggravate / irritate | *Aggravate*: make a bad thing worse. *Irritate*: annoy. |
| all right | Two words. |
| allude / refer; allusion / illusion | An allusion is an indirect mention; a reference is direct. An illusion is a false impression. *Elude* means escape. |
| alternate / alternative | *Alternate*: every other one, or a stand-in. *Alternative*: a choice. |
| among / between | *Among* for a group; *between* for two, or for many considered one to one. |
| and/or | Write *A or B or both*. |
| anticipate / expect | *Expect* for plain expectation; *anticipate* means to act ahead of it. |
| anybody, anyone, everybody, nobody, somebody | One word for "any person"; *any one* means a single item. |
| as good or better than | *as good as X, or better*. |
| as to whether; as yet | *Whether*; *yet*. |
| being (after *regarded as*) | Omit: *regarded as the best*. |
| but (after *doubt*, *help*) | *No doubt that*; *couldn't help seeing*. Avoid one *but* answering another. |
| can / may | *Can*: is able. *May*: is allowed. |
| case, character, nature | Usually padding: *in most cases the rotors failed* → *most rotors failed*; *of a hostile nature* → *hostile*. |
| certainly, very, so (as boosters) | Cut, or choose a stronger word. |
| claim | Means to assert a right; don't use it for *say* or *declare*. |
| compare to / compare with | *To*: show likeness between unlike things. *With*: show differences between like things. |
| comprise | The whole comprises the parts; the parts make up the whole. Never *is comprised of*. |
| consider | No *as* when it means "believe to be"; *as* when it means "examine". |
| contact (verb) | Prefer the specific verb: *call, email, message, meet*. |
| cope | *Cope with* something. |
| could care less | The idiom is *couldn't care less*. |
| currently | Redundant with a present-tense verb. |
| different than | *Different from*. |
| disinterested / uninterested | *Disinterested*: impartial. *Uninterested*: not interested. |
| divided into / composed of | A thing is divided into parts it can be cut into; it is composed of its materials. |
| due to | Means "caused by" after a linking verb (*the crash was due to lag*). Elsewhere use *because of*. |
| each and every | Pick one. |
| enormity | Great wickedness, not great size. |
| enthuse | *Be enthusiastic*. |
| etc. | Only when the reader can fill in the rest; never after *such as* or *for example*. In formal writing, name the item. |
| fact | Only for what can be checked, never for a judgement. |
| facility, factor, feature | Say the thing itself: *the prison*, *better training won it*, *what she sang and how*. |
| farther / further | *Farther*: distance. *Further*: degree, time, quantity. |
| fewer / less | *Fewer* for things you count; *less* for amount. |
| fix | Means to make firm or set; casual for *repair* or *arrange*. |
| folk | Plural sense already; *folks* for family is casual. |
| fortuitous | By chance, not *lucky*. |
| get | *Have*, not *have got*, in writing. |
| gratuitous | Unearned or uncalled for. |
| he is a man who; this is a subject that | Padding: *he is ambitious*; *this subject*. |
| hopefully | Means "full of hope". For "I hope", write *I hope*. |
| however | First in a sentence it means "in whatever way". For "nevertheless", put it after the first phrase. |
| imply / infer | The writer implies; the reader infers. |
| importantly | *More important*, or rephrase. |
| in regard to | Not *in regards to*; *as regards* is fine. |
| in terms of; in the last analysis; along these lines | Padding; rephrase. |
| inside of | *Inside*, except to mean "in less than" (*inside of a minute*). |
| interesting, funny | Make it so instead of labeling it. |
| irregardless | *Regardless*. |
| -ize, -oriented, -wise | Use an existing verb or plain phrase: *use*, not *utilize*; *in price*, not *pricewise*. Never turn a noun into a verb with *-ize*. |
| kind of, sort of, type | Only in the literal sense (*a kind of alloy*). *That kind of grid*, not *that type grid*. |
| lay / lie | You lay something down; you lie down. *Lie, lay, lain*; *lay, laid, laid*. |
| leave / let | *Let it run*, not *leave it run*. |
| lend / loan | *Lend* as the verb; *loan* as the noun. |
| like / as | *Like* before nouns; *as* before clauses (*as the log shows*). |
| literally | Only when it is literally true. |
| meaningful, insightful, prestigious, offputting, ongoing | Inflated or vague; say what makes it so, or cut. |
| memento | Not *momento*. |
| most / almost | *Almost everyone*. |
| nauseous / nauseated | *Nauseated*: feeling sick. *Nauseous*: causing it. |
| nice | Vague; keep for "precise" (*a nice distinction*). |
| nor / or | After a negative: *can't eat or sleep*, *neither eat nor sleep*. |
| nouns as verbs (*gift, host, chair, debut*) | Suspect each one; use the ordinary verb. |
| one | *One must watch one's step*, not *his step*. |
| one of the most | A worn formula; say what it is. |
| partially / partly | *Partially*: to a degree (a condition). *Partly*: in part (a piece of a whole). |
| participle vs gerund | *the server's restarting*, not *the server restarting*, when the action is the subject. |
| people / persons | *People* for a population; not with exact numbers. |
| personalize, personally | Usually unnecessary. |
| possess | *Have* or *own*. |
| presently | *Soon*; use *now* for the present. |
| regretful / regrettable | A mistake is regrettable; a person is regretful. |
| relate (to a person) | Say what you share. |
| respectively | Usually omit and pair the items directly. |
| secondly, thirdly | *Second, third*. |
| split infinitive | Avoid unless the adverb needs the stress, or the unsplit version sounds stiff. |
| state | To set out fully, not just *say*. |
| than | Check nothing is missing: *closer to Ana than to Ben* vs *closer to Ana than Ben is*. |
| thanking you in advance | Ask (*Would you please*), and thank them afterward. |
| that / which | *That* defines (restrictive, no commas); *which* adds a fact (nonrestrictive, commas). |
| the fact is, the truth is, the fact that | State the fact; *the fact that* can nearly always be cut (*since, though, his failure*). |
| the foreseeable future | Vague; give the timeframe. |
| this (alone) | When *this* stands for a whole earlier sentence, add a noun: *this change*. |
| thrust | Keep for physical thrust; otherwise *point* or *aim*. |
| tortuous / torturous | Winding / painful. |
| transpire | Means "become known", not "happen". |
| try and | *Try to*. |
| unique | Has no degrees; not *very unique*. |
| verbal / oral | *Oral* for spoken; *verbal* for in words. |
| while | For "during the time that", or carefully for "although". Not as a loose *and* or *but*; use a semicolon. |
| worth while, worthwhile | Vague praise; say *useful, promising, valuable*. |
| would (habit) | With a time phrase (*every night*), the plain past is enough. Signal clearly when a narrative moves from habit to one specific day. |

## Adapted from the book

- **Singular *they*** is fine for a person of unknown gender and after *everyone, someone, anybody*. The book's alternatives also remain good: make the noun plural, drop the pronoun, or address the reader as *you*.
- ***Shall* / *will***: the book's formal rule is dropped; use *will*.
- ***Data***: follow the project's usage; the singular is standard in technical writing.
- ***Flammable***: now the standard word; the book's preference for *inflammable* is dropped.
